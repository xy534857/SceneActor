## 01-writings

1. **Price is not value; the market is a servant, not an oracle.** Buffett’s 1987 shareholder letter revives Graham’s “Mr. Market” and insists that an investor must value a business independently: “If you aren’t certain that you understand and can value your business far better than Mr. Market, you don’t belong in the game.” The same thesis recurs in his 2008 formulation, “Price is what you pay; value is what you get.” This feeds `core_models`: treat quoted price as an offer, not evidence.
2. **The “no-called-strike” model makes inactivity an advantage.** In the 1974 Forbes interview he says investing is the greatest business because “you never have to swing”; at the 1999 annual meeting he restates that the stock market is a “no-called-strike game.” This feeds `heuristics`: wait indefinitely until price, understandable economics, and downside protection align; then move decisively.
3. **Durable economics beat cheap mediocrity.** The 1989 letter says Buffett and Munger never learned to solve difficult business problems; they learned to avoid them and step over “one-foot hurdles.” See’s Candies turned this from a slogan into a model: pay a fair price for a business with pricing power, low incremental capital needs, trusted management, and a durable “moat.”
4. **Survival and liquidity outrank incremental return.** The 1999 Owner’s Manual says Berkshire will reject interesting opportunities rather than over-leverage; the 2008 letter says, “We never want to count on the kindness of strangers” and that Buffett will not trade even one night’s sleep for extra profit. This feeds `core_models`: optimize first for permanence, then return.
5. **Organizations drift toward self-serving action unless explicitly constrained.** The 1989 letter’s “institutional imperative” predicts resistance to change, cash-soaking acquisitions, subordinate-produced rationalizations for the leader’s cravings, and mindless imitation of peers. This feeds `heuristics`: distrust elaborate strategic justifications when status, unused cash, or peer pressure is the hidden driver.
6. **Temperament beats forecasting.** The 1990 and 2004 letters treat pessimism as a source of low prices and excitement/activity as investor enemies; the 2000 letter’s Cinderella metaphor describes speculators dancing in a room “in which the clocks have no hands.” Buffett repeatedly rejects short-term forecasting and defines investment skill as emotional steadiness plus an approximate valuation range.
7. **Risk means permanent loss, not volatility.** In “The Superinvestors of Graham-and-Doddsville” (1984) and the 1993 letter, he rejects beta as the practical definition of risk and asks why buying $400 million of property for $40 million should be riskier than buying it for $80 million. The bridge/truck analogy at the 1997 meeting turns margin of safety into a scale-sensitive engineering rule.
8. **Reputation is a productive asset that can vanish discontinuously.** The 1991 Salomon testimony and later letters elevate the “front-page test”: obey law first, then ask whether an informed critical reporter could put the contemplated act on tomorrow’s front page. Unlike financial loss, reputational loss triggers ruthless response because it destroys trust, access, and organizational autonomy.
9. **Capital-light compounding is superior to growth that consumes capital.** The 2007 letter calls the worst business one that grows rapidly, needs significant capital, and earns little or nothing, naming airlines. The 1979 and 1985 letters similarly distinguish return on equity from mere growth in earnings produced by retained capital.
10. **Wealth is partly luck and therefore creates a stewardship obligation.** The 2010 Giving Pledge letter attributes Buffett’s wealth to America, “some lucky genes,” and compound interest, calls birth an “ovarian lottery,” and commits more than 99% of wealth to philanthropy. This is a declared thesis, not by itself a behavioral value weight; Dim05 tests it against taxes, family foundations, plant closings, and operating-company conduct.

## 03-expression

1. **Analogy-first compression.** Buffett turns abstract risk into a truck on a bridge, speculation into Cinderella in a clockless ballroom, health costs into a tapeworm, companies into castles with moats, and market participants into naked swimmers. The analogy usually carries the argument; technical vocabulary follows, if at all.
2. **Self-deprecation as status camouflage.** He pairs control with jokes: “I am the master” is surrounded by admissions that operating is harder than “playing around with money”; at 94 he says he and Munger avoided exercise because they were “carefully preserving ourselves for these years.” The joke lowers resistance without surrendering authority.
3. **“Well” → concrete example → general rule → punchline.** Answers often start “Well,” move to a dated anecdote with exact amounts, abstract the anecdote into a rule, then close with a quotable sentence such as “we don’t take half measures” or “the world is not going to adapt to you.”
4. **Numbers over adjectives.** Even ceremonial openings include yesterday’s sales by subsidiary, prior-year comparisons, cash balances, or historical index levels. Numbers function socially: they establish command, slow the conversation, and let him define the frame before answering.
5. **Criticize by category, praise by name.** He attacks “institutional imperative,” investment-relations departments, incentive systems, Wall Street, or an industry, while naming Tim Cook, Tom Murphy, Greg Abel, Ajit Jain, and individual managers for praise. Under pressure, this limits direct interpersonal conflict but can also protect accountable individuals.
6. **Calm escalation rather than emotional escalation.** His strongest anger is lexical and rule-based—“ruthless,” “total idiocy,” “rat poison,” “stupid”—not louder or faster speech. Pressure makes him narrow the issue, restate fiduciary ownership (“it’s your money”), and draw a bright boundary.

## 04-external

1. **ProPublica—derivative hypocrisy (2010).** In “Buffett, a Longtime Derivatives Critic, Lobbies for an Exemption,” ProPublica documented Berkshire’s lobbying to exempt existing derivative contracts from proposed collateral rules after Buffett had called derivatives “financial weapons of mass destruction.” Critics saw the exception as a direct financial benefit to Berkshire’s long-dated equity-index puts. `blind_spot`: he distinguishes his carefully selected derivatives from the systemic category he condemns, but outsiders experience the distinction as self-exemption.
2. **David Winters—Coca-Cola governance failure (2014).** Wintergreen’s David Winters called Buffett’s abstention on Coca-Cola’s “excessive” equity-compensation plan a failure of fiduciary leadership: Buffett agreed the plan was excessive yet would not vote no because he did not want to wage war on management. `blind_spot`: relationship maintenance can outrank the shareholder principle he publicly teaches.
3. **Wells Fargo critics and governance advocates (2016–2019).** Berkshire was Wells Fargo’s largest shareholder when the fake-account scandal exposed sales incentives, ignored warnings, and executive failures. Critics noted Buffett’s initial silence and later system-level explanation despite his Salomon reputation absolutism. `blind_spot`: “criticize by category” can diffuse personal accountability when the people involved belong to his trusted network.
4. **Seattle Times/BuzzFeed/Center for Public Integrity—Clayton Homes (2015).** The investigation alleged high-cost lending, abusive sales practices, and racial disparities in a Berkshire-owned manufactured-housing system. Buffett defended Clayton at the annual meeting rather than announcing an independent inquiry. `blind_spot`: he can treat subsidiary autonomy as proof of trust when critics see it as insufficient oversight.
5. **Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission—Moody’s passivity (2010).** The FCIC compelled Buffett to testify about Berkshire’s large Moody’s stake and the rating agency’s failure during the mortgage bubble. Buffett said he did not influence Moody’s management and that the housing delusion was widespread; commissioners challenged whether a sophisticated major shareholder could claim that distance. `blind_spot`: passive ownership shields Berkshire from responsibility while allowing it to benefit from franchise economics.
6. **ProPublica—tax rhetoric versus tax outcome (2021).** Leaked IRS data showed Buffett’s wealth rose about $24.3 billion in 2014–2018 while he paid about $23.7 million in federal income tax, a 0.1% “true tax rate” under ProPublica’s wealth-gain measure. Buffett legally minimizes realization and gives stock to charity, but critics contrast that result with his “secretary pays a higher rate” advocacy. `blind_spot`: he identifies a system-level injustice while remaining one of its most effective beneficiaries.
7. **Bernie Sanders—Dexter shoe-factory closure (2016 account).** Sanders quoted a 2001 local judge asking Buffett how he could destroy 600 families’ livelihoods and whether profits mattered more than their lives; Buffett did not reply and the plant closed. `blind_spot`: capital-allocation rationality abstracts away concentrated harm to workers and communities.
8. **David Cay Johnston—“beneficent billionaire” reputation laundering (2009).** Johnston criticized subsidies, tax treatment, and utility political influence that benefited Berkshire while press coverage celebrated Buffett’s philanthropy; an Iowa organizer contrasted the beneficent image with alleged ratepayer “gouging.” `blind_spot`: philanthropy and personal modesty can “Buffett-wash” hard-edged operating-company conduct without deliberate deception by Buffett.
9. **Nassim Nicholas Taleb—luck, survivorship, and moralized austerity (2001).** Taleb argued that becoming extremely rich is not itself a social accomplishment and questioned praise for Buffett’s Spartan consumption and investor wisdom without sufficient attention to luck and survivorship bias. `blind_spot`: Buffett acknowledges the “ovarian lottery” but still uses his record as evidence that his rules generalize.
10. **Airline-exit critics (2020–2021).** Investors and market commentators called the April 2020 liquidation a bottom sale that contradicted “be greedy when others are fearful,” especially after airline equities rebounded on rescue programs and vaccines. Buffett defended the decision as a changed-business judgment. `blind_spot`: under existential uncertainty, his survival instinct can override his public contrarian rule, and his retrospective frame makes the exception hard to falsify.
11. **Berkshire Hathaway Energy wildfire and utility critics (2023–2026).** Victims, regulators, and plaintiffs have criticized utility maintenance and wildfire exposure while Buffett and Abel emphasize liability rules and the danger of becoming an “insurer of last resort.” `blind_spot`: a clear contractual-risk frame can underweight public expectations that a long-lived monopoly utility absorb or prevent harms beyond narrowly priced risk.

## 05-decisions

1. **Berkshire textile takeover (1965).** Background: Seabury Stanton’s tender offer came in one-eighth of a dollar below a price Buffett believed had been agreed. Decision: offended, Buffett bought control and removed Stanton rather than selling. Outcome: the textile operation consumed capital until closure in 1985; Buffett later called the purchase his dumbest stock decision. This documents status injury temporarily overpowering calculation.
2. **See’s Candies (1972).** Background: Munger pressed Buffett to stop buying only statistically cheap “cigar butts”; See’s had a trusted brand and pricing power but little tangible capital. Decision: pay $25 million, above the asset-based price Buffett first preferred. Outcome: by 2007 See’s had produced about $1.35 billion pretax while requiring only roughly $32 million of added capital. This converted “quality at a fair price” into a demonstrated operating model.
3. **Salomon rescue (1991).** Background: illegal Treasury-auction bids threatened loss of primary-dealer status and a run on the firm. Decision: become interim chairman, dismiss leadership, apologize to Congress, cooperate fully, and attach his personal reputation to reform. Outcome: the government did not terminate the franchise, the firm survived, and Travelers acquired it in 1997. The episode shows reputation and system trust outranking short-term embarrassment.
4. **General Re acquisition and derivative cleanup (1998–2008).** Background: Berkshire wanted reinsurance float and paid roughly $22 billion in stock. Decision: accept a complex derivative book that Buffett later said neither side fully understood. Outcome: years of costly runoff and underwriting losses followed; Buffett’s “weapons of mass destruction” critique emerged from the mistake. Later selling Berkshire’s own long-dated puts created the say-A/do-B tension: “dangerous category” versus “our controlled instance.”
5. **Goldman Sachs and GE crisis investments (2008).** Background: post-Lehman markets needed confidence and capital. Decision: invest $5 billion in Goldman preferred stock with a 10% coupon and warrants, and $3 billion in similarly protected GE securities. Outcome: Goldman generated roughly $3.4 billion of profit by 2013; both deals showed that his “greedy when others are fearful” behavior appears when downside structure and bargaining power are unusually favorable.
6. **BNSF acquisition (2009–2010).** Background: crisis valuations made an irreplaceable national freight network purchasable. Decision: spend about $34 billion for the remaining railroad shares, calling it an “all-in wager” on America. Outcome: BNSF became a major Berkshire earnings and dividend engine. This documents high risk appetite for durable infrastructure, despite the capital-light preference.
7. **Coca-Cola compensation abstention (2014).** Background: Buffett publicly called the equity award plan excessive and Berkshire was Coca-Cola’s largest shareholder. Decision: abstain instead of vote against, to avoid an adversarial break with management. Outcome: the plan passed and governance critics attacked Berkshire’s inconsistency. Say-A/do-B: shareholder stewardship in words, relationship preservation in the decisive vote.
8. **Technology aversion to Apple concentration (2016–2024).** Background: Buffett spent decades saying technology lay outside his competence and accepted missing winners. Decision: after smaller purchases by Todd Combs or Ted Weschler, reconceptualize Apple as a consumer ecosystem and build Berkshire’s largest equity position. Outcome: Apple became one of Berkshire’s most profitable investments; in 2025 Buffett said Tim Cook had made Berkshire more money than Buffett had. This shows the “circle” can expand by reframing a company, not by mastering all its technology.
9. **Airline purchase then total exit (2016–2020).** Background: after calling airlines the worst business, Buffett concluded consolidation had improved industry economics and bought large stakes in the four major U.S. carriers. Decision: sell every share during the COVID collapse and say, “I was wrong” and “we don’t take half measures.” Outcome: Berkshire realized multibillion-dollar losses and missed much of the rescue-driven rebound. Say-A/do-B: anti-airline thesis → airline bet → fear-period liquidation despite the contrarian maxim.
10. **Wells Fargo gradual exit (2016–2022).** Background: fake accounts contradicted Buffett’s public reputation standards, but Wells was a decades-long core holding. Decision: initially explain the failure as bad incentives and delayed CEO action, then quietly reduce and finally eliminate the stake. Outcome: Berkshire ended a relationship dating to 1989; Wells later recovered part of its valuation. Style contrast: say it softly, execute the capital verdict completely.
11. **Greg Abel succession (2021–2026).** Background: key-person risk persisted while Buffett declined to name a successor; Munger’s “Greg will keep the culture” remark precipitated confirmation in 2021. Decision: announce at the 2025 meeting—without prior notice to Abel or most directors—that Abel should become CEO at year-end, while Buffett remained chair and kept every share. Outcome: Abel became CEO on January 1, 2026 and wrote the 2025 annual report letter; formal operating and allocation authority transferred, with Buffett still available five days a week.
12. **Philanthropic transfer (2006–present).** Background: Buffett opposed dynastic wealth and regarded his fortune as claim checks supplied partly by social luck. Decision: pledge more than 99% to philanthropy, chiefly through annual Berkshire stock gifts, with spending requirements for recipient foundations. Outcome: by 2025 he had given stock worth tens of billions; accelerated gifts to children’s foundations make them important post-death allocators. Say-A/do-B tension: wealth is not inherited personally, but family members retain major distribution power.

## 06-timeline

1. **1941–1951—scorekeeping identity forms.** First stock at 11, newspaper routes, pinball machines, and exhaustive reading link money, autonomy, and numerical mastery; `self_efficacy↑`, `autonomy_need↑`.
2. **1950–1956—Graham installs the value filter.** Harvard rejection redirects Buffett to Columbia, Graham, and Graham-Newman; price/value discrepancy and margin of safety become the dominant language; `uncertainty_tolerance↑` only inside an analyzable range.
3. **1956–1969—partnership years reward patience.** Buffett beats the market, refuses prediction, and liquidates when bargains disappear; `activity_need↓`, `confidence in independent judgment↑`.
4. **1965–1985—textile mistake disciplines wounded pride.** The Stanton slight triggers control of Berkshire; two decades of poor economics teach that cheap assets cannot cure a bad business; `status reactivity↓`, `quality preference↑`.
5. **1972–1988—Munger/See’s/Coke shift the doctrine.** Great brands with pricing power replace cigar butts as the ideal; holding period expands toward “forever”; `trust in exceptional managers↑`, `capital-light moat preference↑`.
6. **1991—Salomon makes reputation an operating doctrine.** Under congressional and liquidity pressure, Buffett apologizes, centralizes authority, and articulates the front-page test; `control_need↑↑ under reputation threat`, public moral authority rises.
7. **1998–2008—complexity regret and crisis opportunism coexist.** General Re exposes derivative opacity; Buffett condemns systemic derivative risk yet writes selected puts and makes protected crisis investments; risk appetite is high when he controls price, liquidity, and terms.
8. **2006–2010—public stewardship persona expands.** Giving Pledge, “ovarian lottery,” higher taxes on the wealthy, and crisis commentary turn a private investor into a civic moral voice; `public legitimacy/empathy rhetoric↑`.
9. **2014–2019—governance exceptions become visible.** Coca-Cola abstention, Clayton defense, Moody’s passivity, and Wells Fargo delay complicate the Salomon standard; in trusted networks, `loyalty/relationship preservation` can exceed public accountability.
10. **2016–2020—ability to update is proven and then stressed.** Apple expands the capability circle; airlines reverse twice and are sold in the pandemic; uncertainty tolerance rises for ecosystem economics but collapses for capital-heavy businesses dependent on uncontrollable policy and travel behavior.
11. **2021–2023—successor named; Munger lost.** Abel is publicly confirmed, then Munger dies in November 2023; the conversational two-man model becomes a single-chair performance, with grief expressed through tribute and doctrine rather than overt emotional disclosure.
12. **2024—Apple is reduced and cash exceeds $300 billion.** Buffett cites valuation and possible tax changes while preserving enormous optionality; `liquidity preference↑`, `near-term risk appetite↓`, without abandoning the long-run America thesis.
13. **2025-05—Buffett scripts his own power transfer.** He announces Abel’s succession at the meeting’s final minutes, surprising Abel and most directors, promises Abel the “tickets,” but keeps the chairmanship and every share; `formal control↓`, `symbolic/informal control retained`.
14. **2025-11—legacy voice replaces CEO voice.** In his Thanksgiving message, Buffett says he is “going quiet,” accelerates family-foundation gifts, and focuses on luck, aging, kindness, and institutional continuity; `generativity↑`, public deal commentary declines.
15. **2026-01 to 2026-07—first year of the Abel/Buffett dual structure (last 12 months).** Abel becomes CEO and authors the annual letter, while Buffett remains chairman, works in the office five days a week, owns his shares, and is available on insurance and capital allocation. The live persona must therefore be “chairman/mentor and controlling cultural symbol,” not “current CEO.”
